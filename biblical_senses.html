Spiritual Senses

How the five senses God gave us can teach us to distinguish good from evil — what helps us grow from what harms us.

The Five Senses Spiritual Senses — Hebrews 5:14
Hebrews 5:14
"But solid food is for the mature, who by constant use have trained themselves to distinguish good from evil."
New International Version (NIV)

The Biblical Principle

Training to Distinguish Good from Evil

The writer of Hebrews uses a powerful image: spiritual maturity is like moving from milk to solid food. A baby cannot eat steak — not because steak is bad, but because the baby's digestive system is not ready. In the same way, new believers start with the basics of faith, but mature Christians train themselves to make wise decisions.

How do they train? "By constant use." Just as a musician practices scales every day, or an athlete trains their body daily, spiritual discernment is built through repeated, intentional practice. The more we exercise our spiritual senses, the sharper they become.

God gave us five physical senses — sight, hearing, smell, taste, and touch — and each one is a window to the world. What we allow through those windows shapes our thoughts, our character, and ultimately our relationship with God. Learning to filter what we see, hear, smell, taste, and touch is the beginning of wisdom.

The Connection: Physical Senses → Spiritual Discernment

  • Physical senses help us navigate the material world — they tell us what is safe and what is dangerous.
  • Spiritual senses help us navigate the moral world — they tell us what is good and what is harmful to our soul.
  • Just as a damaged sense (blindness, deafness) limits our interaction with the physical world, a neglected spiritual sense limits our ability to make wise choices.
  • The good news: spiritual senses, like physical ones, can be trained and strengthened through practice, Scripture, prayer, and community.

Sight — What You Look At

Physical Sense: Sight

Your eyes are the windows to the world. The cornea and lens focus light onto the retina, where rods and cones convert images into electrical signals the brain can interpret. But your eyes do not just receive — they choose. You decide where to look and what to focus on.

Spiritual Parallel: Discernment of Vision

What we choose to look at shapes our inner world. Just as the eye can be damaged by staring at the sun, the soul can be damaged by constantly looking at what is harmful. Scripture says, "I will not look with approval on anything that is vile" (Psalm 101:3).

Sight and Discernment

  • Good (healthy vision): Looking at what is true, noble, pure, lovely, admirable — things that are excellent or praiseworthy (Philippians 4:8).
  • Evil (damaged vision): Consuming content that promotes violence, lust, greed, or hatred — content that desensitizes the conscience.
  • Training the eye: Practice choosing what you watch, read, and scroll through. Ask: "Does this build me up or tear me down?"

Challenge

  1. What is the last thing you watched that did not honor God? How did it affect your mood or thoughts?
  2. Can you identify one change you could make this week in what you watch or read?
  3. Read Philippians 4:8. Write down five things that pass this test.

Hearing — What You Listen To

Physical Sense: Hearing

Sound waves enter through the pinna, travel down the ear canal, vibrate the eardrum, and are amplified by three tiny bones before reaching the cochlea. Hair cells convert vibrations into signals the brain interprets as speech, music, or noise. Your ear does not filter — your mind does.

Spiritual Parallel: Discernment of Hearing

What we listen to enters our minds and takes root. The words of others — friends, teachers, media — shape our beliefs and attitudes. Jesus said, "Everyone who hears these words of mine and puts them into practice is like a wise man" (Matthew 7:24).

Hearing and Discernment

  • Good (healthy hearing): Listening to truth, wise counsel, worship music, Scripture, and encouragement that builds faith.
  • Evil (damaged hearing): Filling your ears with gossip, lies, complaints, vulgar language, or teachings that distort the truth.
  • Training the ear: Be intentional about what you listen to. Ask: "Does this voice align with God's character?"

Challenge

  1. What kind of music or podcasts do you listen to most? Do they lift your spirit or lower it?
  2. Is there someone in your life whose words always leave you feeling discouraged? How can you protect your hearing?
  3. Read Romans 10:17. How does faith come by hearing?

Smell — What You Are Near

Physical Sense: Smell

When you breathe in, air molecules reach the olfactory epithelium at the top of the nasal cavity. About 400 types of olfactory receptors detect different molecules and send signals directly to the brain's limbic system — the center of emotion and memory. That is why a smell can instantly transport you to a distant place or a forgotten moment.

Spiritual Parallel: Discernment of Atmosphere

Smell is about what surrounds you. You cannot always see the air you breathe, but it enters your body and affects you deeply. In the same way, the spiritual atmosphere you place yourself in — the environments, relationships, and habits you surround yourself with — shapes who you become.

Smell and Discernment

  • Good (healthy smell): Surrounding yourself with environments that promote peace, growth, worship, and genuine community.
  • Evil (damaged smell): Staying in toxic environments — places of dishonesty, manipulation, substance abuse, or spiritual emptiness — even when nothing "visible" seems wrong.
  • Training the nose: Learn to detect when something is "off" even before you can explain why. That intuition is the Holy Spirit's voice. Trust it.

Challenge

  1. Think of a situation where you felt something was "not right" but could not explain it. What was that feeling?
  2. What environments do you spend the most time in? Are they helping or hurting your spiritual health?
  3. Read 2 Corinthians 2:15-16. What does it mean to be the "fragrance of Christ"?

Taste — What You Feed On

Physical Sense: Taste

When you eat, saliva dissolves chemicals from food. Taste cells inside your taste buds detect five basic flavors: sweet, sour, salty, bitter, and umami. But taste is not just about the tongue — the brain combines taste with smell, memory, and emotion to create the full experience of flavor. What you eat literally becomes part of your body.

Spiritual Parallel: Discernment of Input

Taste is about what you consume — not just food, but ideas, entertainment, relationships, and experiences. What you take in shapes your character. The Psalmist declared, "Taste and see that the Lord is good" (Psalm 34:8). When you sample God's goodness, your palate changes — you begin to prefer what is wholesome.

Taste and Discernment

  • Good (healthy taste): Feeding on Scripture, prayer, worship, acts of service, and relationships that challenge you to grow.
  • Evil (damaged taste): Consuming entertainment, habits, or relationships that numb your conscience, exploit others, or pull you away from God's purpose.
  • Training the palate: Start with small spiritual habits — daily Bible reading, weekly worship, serving someone in need. Over time, your taste changes. What once seemed boring becomes life-giving.

Challenge

  1. What are you "feeding on" most — spiritually speaking? (books, shows, conversations, habits)
  2. Read 1 Peter 2:2-3. Why does the writer compare God's word to milk for a newborn?
  3. Try this week: replace 30 minutes of one habit with time in Scripture or prayer. Notice how you feel afterward.

Touch — What You Reach For

Physical Sense: Touch

Your skin is the largest organ of your body. Different receptors detect pressure, temperature, pain, and vibration. Touch is the most intimate sense — it requires contact. You cannot touch something without being changed by it, even slightly. A handshake, a hug, a burn — every touch leaves a mark.

Spiritual Parallel: Discernment of Action

Touch is about what you engage with — the actions you take, the things you put your hands to, the relationships you enter. Proverbs says, "Do not lay your hand on anyone without thought" (Proverbs 6:30, paraphrased). Every action has consequences. What you reach for reveals what you value.

Touch and Discernment

  • Good (healthy touch): Using your hands to build, create, help, heal, and bless others. Reaching out in generosity, kindness, and service.
  • Evil (damaged touch): Using your hands to take, manipulate, harm, steal, or control. Reaching for what is not yours or using power to exploit the weak.
  • Training the hands: Ask yourself before every action: "Will this action reflect God's love or my selfishness?" Practice doing good until it becomes your default.

Challenge

  1. What did you do with your hands this week that you are proud of? What do you regret?
  2. Read James 2:14-17. Why is faith without action called "dead"?
  3. Plan one intentional act of service this week — use your hands to bless someone.
